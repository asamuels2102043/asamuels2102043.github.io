The Richest Man in Babylon by George S. Clason (1926)

See end of page for my thoughts

Seven Cures For A Lean Purse

In this chapter, the King contests his Councellor. He asks him why is it that all men could not be rich, and what is it then that makes a man rich. His Councellor turns, wisely, and suggests to his King, shouldn't they inquire of the richest man in all these lands? So, it is then we are again introduced Arkad; and here, we learn about the seven cures for a lean purse, which Arkad states are:

  1. "Start thy purse to fattening."
    2. Here, Arkad essentially says to save. He says if a man earns 10 gold, to save the tenth gold and use the other nine for things of import.
  2. "Control thy expenditures."
    3. For this cure, Arkad says for the nine gold that remain for oneself, to separate necessity from indulgence. Essentially, in your spending, limit what is not required about ones home, and spend mainly on what is absolute essential for the home.
  3. "Make thy gold multiply."
    4. This is one of the first steps to investments. Arkad says to put those saving to work, to make loan of your gold and what I believe he was alluding to, with interest. One fourth the value of the capital each fourth year added onto it.
  4. "Guard thy treasures from loss."
    5. Arkad expresses not simply that money should be stored securely, but that investements made must not outpace what one can pay back. Guarding your treasures from loss in this way is about making sure you're not out of money to pay back what is owed.
  5. "Make of thy dwelling a profitable investment."
    6. For this cure, Arkad says to men that they must make use of their land and put it to work in the same way they should put their gold to work. The first step is owning your home, that having complete creative liberty over ones home is invaluable, and allows for step two: renting your home or space. As we know in the modern world, rentals are highly profitable, whether for dwelling, farming, storage, or corporate purposes.
  6. "Insure a future income."
    7. Building on our first and third cures, Arkad tells us to put aside gold for our future days, that we may reap a handsome sum when we grow old should be loan a large sum to any one man of good character, and he should return in installments trifling sums of money each month or each year.
  7. "Increase they ability to earn."
    8. For our final cure, Arkad imparts that we must improve our skills of trade, that we must better ourselves for the benefit of our purse; that if one should become more than simple amiable at ones given task, they should be able to bargain for more gold, that their skills are worth more than what they could offer before.

Five Laws of Gold

In this chapter, we meet Kalabab, a camel trader who has employed 27 guards and helpers on his journey back to Babylon for accompaniment. Short of a day's journey, Kalabab decides to reward the men for their service, with a tale. He first poses the question to them,"A bag heavy with gold or a clay tablet carved with words of wisdom; if thou hadst thy choice, which wouldst thou choose?" Naturally, they chose the gold, to which Kalabab chides them, and tells a tale of Arkad's son, Nomasir, who was given a stone tablet inscribed with five laws of gold, sent out to prove his ability to manage wealth. He gambles away his wealth in Nineveh, and once he is left with nothing, he takes to the tablet, and learns, and practices these laws, and returns to his father a richer man; he claims proudly that indeed, wisdom is worth far more than gold. The five laws are as follows:

  1. "Gold cometh gladly and in increasing quantity to any man who will put by not less than one-tenth or his earnings to create an estate for his future and that or his family."
    2. Kalabab tells us that gold beckons gladly to the man who invests wisely, and spares a tenth consistently of his earned gold. He testifies himself of its truth, that the more he accumulates, the more he gains.
  2. "Gold laboureth diligently and contentedly for the wise owner who finds for it profitable employment, multiplying even as the flocks of the field."
    3. Investment. Putting gold to work, profitable employment, may increase its amount and value. Kalabab is saying for any man that has a sum of gold set aside, opportunity will come for it to multiply, as we've learned with Arkad's teachings; loans, interests, investing in profitable business.
  3. "Gold clingeth to the protection of the cautious owner who invests it under the advice of men wise in its handling."
    4. Again, this is not simply about storing ones gold in secure places, as that is very important. This is more about ensuring that your gold is wisely invested, wisely saved, and wisely spent. Securing your gold is about making sure that in every capacity it is multiplying, not decreasing. Kalabab says to seek the wisdom of men wise in handling gold, to learn not to jeopardize your treasure.
  4. "Gold slippeth away from the man who invests it in businesses or purposes with which he is not familiar or which are not approved by those skilled in its keep."
    5. Much like the previous two laws, this is about being wise as to where your gold is invested, to invest only in trustworthy causes, businesses. Trustworthy means something you are familiar with, that is of good repute. Kalabab says to seek counsel of men skilled in the ways of gold, experienced men, that inexperience leads to poor judgement which leads to loss of gold.
  5. "Gold flees the man who would force it to impossible earnings or who followeth the alluring advice of tricksters and schemers or who trusts it to his own inexperience and romantic desires in investment."
    6. There are many who would see a man rich in gold and sing to him songs of fortune and notoriety for their own gain, and for his loss; Kalabab calls them schemers, tricksters. He says again to trust the wise men, that they are experienced in these losses, in unprofitable investments [emdash] to not tie up ones money in such investments for the tale of gain.

Takeaways, Life Alignment

The things I can align to my life:
  • Money saved is money valued - If I am to save religiously and in meaninful amounts, that I will reap a fine sum at a later date.
  • I also learn that if I not only save this money, but invest it wisely where it is sure to grow, then I can expect more than what I would have gotten with money just sitting around.
  • I've also taken from the chapters that I shouldn't let just any investment that sounds profitable take my money, but to be wise, and to listen to the words of wise and trustworthy people who are skilled with money.
My biggest takeaway from this book is that although it is an old book, money hasn't changed, it still operates in the exact same way, and if we could only learn to separate the tricksters and schemers from the wise and skilled, that we could all increase our wealth.
